## 2.1 The resource hierarchy

= dia | The tree | Organization, folders, projects, resources
p2-hierarchy
N: Here is the tree. At the top, the organization: the root, one per Cloud Identity or Workspace customer, or one standalone organization. Below it, folders, which are optional and nestable up to ten levels: departments, environments, teams. Then projects, which can hang from a folder, or directly from the organization. And inside projects, resources: a V-M, a bucket, a Cloud SQL instance, a cluster, a topic. Follow the arrows downward, because policy travels in that direction. Four rules follow from this picture, and each one is examined directly.

= table | Four rules from the tree | Each one is examined directly
# | Rule | What it decides
1 | **IAM allow policies inherit downward and are additive** | A folder grant reaches every project in it, including later ones
2 | **Organization policies inherit downward too** | A lower node can replace, or for list constraints merge. It binds Owners
3 | **The project is the boundary** | Billing link, quota, API enablement and, by default, networking
4 | **Grant at the smallest node that covers the future** | "All of Finance's projects, including new ones" is one folder binding
N: Rule one. I-A-M allow policies are inherited downward and are additive. A grant on a folder is effective on every project in it, including projects created later. There is no most specific wins. Rule two. Organization policies inherit downward too. The constraint is the rule type that Google defines. The organization policy is your setting of that constraint on a node. A lower node can replace it, or for list constraints, merge with it. And unlike I-A-M, it binds Owners too. Rule three. The project is the boundary for billing linkage, quota, A-P-I enablement and, by default, networking. So stopping one team exhausting another's quota means separate projects. Rule four. Grant at the smallest node that covers the requirement, including its future. All of Finance's projects, including new ones, is one binding on the Finance folder, not forty bindings on forty projects.

= table | Project identifiers | Three identifiers, and only one is yours to change
Identifier | Mutable? | Notes
Project **ID** | **No** | Globally unique, chosen at creation, 6–30 characters. What almost every command takes
Project **name** | Yes | A display label; not unique
Project **number** | No | Assigned by Google; appears in default and Google-managed service account addresses
> `gcloud` accepts the ID or the number, never the display name.
N: A project has three identifiers. The project I-D is globally unique, you choose it at creation, and it can never change. Almost every command takes it. The project name is only a display label: you can change it, and it need not be unique. The project number is assigned by Google, and you will see it inside the addresses of default and Google-managed service accounts. gcloud accepts the I-D or the number, but never the display name.

## 2.2 Creating the organization: Cloud Identity, Workspace, or standalone

= dia new | Two routes | You never create an organization with a command
p2-two-routes
N: An organization resource is created for you. You never create an organization with a command. And now there are two routes to one. The classic route: verify a domain with Cloud Identity or Google Workspace, and the organization appears when that customer creates the first project or billing account. It is named after the domain, and it holds your managed users and groups. The new route, added in the June twenty twenty-six guide, is the standalone organization. It is created automatically when a new Free Trial customer signs in to the console with a Google account and accepts the terms. It is named after the user, never after a domain.

= list new | Standalone organizations | A real hierarchy, without a domain or a super admin
- A real hierarchy: folders, org policies, org-level IAM
- The signing-up user becomes **Organization Owner**
- Only for **new Free Trial customers**; **one organization per user account**
- The name **cannot be a domain name**, e.g. `lara-brown-org`
- People join by **invitation, accepted within 30 days**
- A service account can never be an owner; at least one active Google account must remain
> External sign-in for a standalone org goes through Workforce Identity Federation.
N: Here are the facts to hold about standalone organizations. They give a new customer a real hierarchy, with folders, organization policies and org-level I-A-M, without a Cloud Identity super administrator or a verified domain. The user who signs up becomes Organization Owner. They are available only to new Free Trial customers, not existing accounts, and there is one organization per user account. The name cannot be a domain name, since domain names are reserved for Cloud Identity and Workspace organizations. Other people are added by invitation, which they must accept within thirty days. A service account can never be an organization owner, and at least one active Google account must remain one. And Workforce Identity Federation, in part three, is how a standalone organization lets people sign in with an external identity provider.

= panel watch | Two administrators, two systems | Super admin versus Organization Administrator
*"Create users, reset passwords, manage groups"* is the **Admin console**, and a Cloud Identity or Workspace admin role.
*"Grant IAM roles on folders and projects"* is the **Organization Administrator** IAM role.
Neither implies the other. Grant org-level roles to **groups** such as `gcp-organization-admins@`.
N: In a Cloud Identity or Workspace organization, there are two kinds of administrator, in two different systems. The super administrator lives in the Admin console, and manages users, groups and domain settings. The Organization Administrator is an I-A-M role, which manages I-A-M on the resource hierarchy. Neither implies the other. So create users and reset passwords points to the Admin console. Grant roles on folders and projects points to the I-A-M role. Google's set-up guidance is to grant org-level roles to groups, not to individuals.

= panel note | Setting up cloud networking | Google Cloud Setup: the guided foundation
The console's guided flow for a new organization: identity and groups, administrative access, billing, the folder hierarchy, a starter network (a Shared VPC host with subnets), central logging and monitoring, and a baseline of security org policies.
It can apply the result directly, or hand it to you as **Terraform**.
N: Google Cloud Setup is the console's guided flow for a new organization. It takes you through identity and groups, administrative access, billing, the folder hierarchy, a starter network with a Shared V-P-C host and subnets, central logging and monitoring, and a baseline of security organization policies. It can apply the result directly, or give it to you as Terraform. This is the practical meaning of the guide's phrase, setting up cloud networking, in section one. It means the foundation network, not individual firewall rules.

## 2.3 Folders and projects in practice

= cmd sm | Commands | Task to command, for the verbs the exam tests
Task | Command
Create a folder | gcloud resource-manager folders create --display-name=finance --organization=ORG_ID

N: The commands. Create a folder with gcloud resource-manager folders create. Create a project in that folder with gcloud projects create, dash dash folder. Moving a project is on the beta track: gcloud beta projects move. Deleting a project does not destroy it right away. It starts a thirty-day pending deletion period. = dia | Best practice | Separate projects for production and non-production
p2-envs
N: Here is the blueprint Google's own foundation design builds, and the one to picture whenever a stem mentions environments. A folder called common holds the platform team's projects: one Shared V-P-C host for production, a separate host for non-production, a logging project and a billing export project. A production folder holds the production service projects, with strict organization policies. A non-production folder holds development and staging. Every environment is its own project, and production and non-production do not even share a network.

= table sm | Why separate projects | Every boundary follows the project
Why | What goes wrong in a shared project
**IAM is inherited and additive** | One Editor grant covers dev **and** prod; nothing subtracts prod
**Quota is per project** | A dev load test exhausts production's CPUs or API quota
**Org policies differ per folder** | A policy cannot target half a project
**Billing** | Each environment's cost is a project in the reports, not a labelling exercise
**Blast radius** | One mistake, one leaked key, one deleted project takes both
> Routes and firewall rules belong to a VPC, so prod and non-prod also get **separate Shared VPC host projects**.
N: Why does every guide insist on this? Because every boundary follows the project. I-A-M is inherited and additive, so a single Editor grant in a shared project covers development and production alike, and nothing can subtract production. Quota is per project, so a load test in development can starve production. Organization policies are set per folder, and cannot target half a project. Each environment's cost shows up as its own project in the billing reports. And the blast radius of a mistake, a leaked key or a deleted project stays inside one environment. The same reasoning applies to the network: routes and firewall rules belong to a V-P-C, so production and non-production get separate Shared V-P-C host projects.

= panel tip | Spot the environment question | Separate projects, not clever IAM
*"Developers need broad rights in dev but none in production"*, *"a test must never use production's quota"*, *"show production cost separately"*.
All three: **separate projects under separate folders**. Not IAM conditions, not labels, not per-environment subnets in one project.
V: Developers need rights in dev but none in production. Separate projects, under separate folders. Not conditions, not labels.

= dia | Assured Workloads | A compliance boundary applied to a folder
p2-assured
N: Assured Workloads, explicitly. It creates a folder with a control package applied to it. Every project created inside that folder inherits the package's guardrails: data residency, which Google personnel may access the data for support, key management, and which services can be enabled at all. Google enforces them through organization policies and other controls, and then monitors the folder and reports violations. Notice the grey box on the left: a project created at the organization root, outside the folder, inherits none of it.

Family | Examples | Controls
**Regional data boundaries** | EU Data Boundary · EU Data Boundary and Support · US, Canada, Japan and others | Location; with *and Support*, also local support personnel
**Regulatory data boundaries** | FedRAMP Moderate and High · IL2, IL4, IL5 · CJIS · ITAR · IRS 1075 · HIPAA controls | Certified sets: location, personnel, keys, allowed services
**Sovereign Controls by Partners** | A local partner holds keys and approves access | Residency, EKM or HSM keys, Key Access Justifications
> `gcloud assured workloads create --compliance-regime=eu-data-boundary-and-support ...` · packages were renamed in June 2025, so expect either name.
N: Control packages come in three families. Regional data boundaries keep data in a country or region, and the and Support variants also restrict support to personnel located there. Regulatory data boundaries are certified sets, such as FedRAMP Moderate and High, the Impact Levels, C-J-I-S and ITAR, which add personnel checks, keys and an allowed list of services. And Sovereign Controls by Partners let a local partner hold keys and approve access. The packages were renamed in June twenty twenty-five, so a stem may use the older name, E-U Regions and Support.

## 2.5 Quotas and limits

= table | Three kinds of ceiling | Allocation quota, rate quota, system limit
Kind | Caps | Example | When hit
**Allocation quota** | How much may exist at once | vCPUs, GPUs, static IPs per region | Creation fails with a quota error
**Rate quota** | Requests per interval | API reads per minute | `429 RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED`; retry with backoff
**System limit** | A fixed maximum | Maximum labels per resource | Redesign, do not request
N: Three kinds of ceiling. An allocation quota caps how much may exist at once, such as V-C-P-Us per region, G-P-Us per region, or static I-P addresses. When you hit it, creation fails with a quota error. A rate quota caps requests per interval, such as A-P-I reads per minute. When you hit it, you get four-two-nine, resource exhausted, and you retry with exponential backoff. A system limit is a fixed maximum that cannot be adjusted. For that, you redesign. You do not file a request.

= list | Working with quotas | Per project, often per region
- **Quotas are per project**, and many are **per region**
- GPUs have both a per-region and an all-regions quota
- Need more? **Request an increase in Cloud Quotas**, for that quota, in that project
- The **quota adjuster** raises eligible quotas automatically as usage nears them; it only raises
- A **quota usage alert** is a Cloud Monitoring alerting policy, not a budget
> Spreading a workload across extra projects to dodge a quota is the distractor.
N: Quotas are per project, and many are per region. G-P-Us have both a per-region quota and an all-regions quota, and you can hit either one. When a legitimate workload needs more, the answer is to request an increase for that quota, in that project, in Cloud Quotas. Or let the quota adjuster raise eligible quotas automatically as usage approaches them. A usage alert from the Quotas page is a Cloud Monitoring alerting policy, because a budget watches money, not quota. Spreading a workload across extra projects to dodge a quota is the distractor. And a note for Free Trial accounts: they cannot use G-P-Us. The fix is to upgrade to a paid billing account, not a quota request.

= th | A distinction worth memorising | A quota is a <em>hard stop</em>. A budget is only an <em>alert</em>. | You can deliberately lower a quota, such as BigQuery query usage per day, to cap consumption.
V: A quota is a hard stop. A budget is only an alert.
[pause: 0.6]
N: You can also lower a quota on purpose. For example, lower BigQuery's query usage per day quota to cap consumption. That is the answer to, make it impossible to exceed, where a budget is not.

## 2.6 Billing accounts and the roles around them

= table | Billing accounts | What pays for what
Fact | Detail
A project links to | **at most one** billing account
A billing account pays for | **many** projects, even across organizations
Self-serve | Card or bank account, charged automatically
Invoiced | Monthly invoice, for eligible customers
Unlinking billing | **Stops paid services**, and resources may be deleted
N: A Cloud Billing account pays for projects. A project is linked to at most one billing account, and a billing account can pay for many projects, even across organizations. There are two kinds. Self-serve uses a card or bank account, charged automatically. Invoiced sends a monthly invoice, for eligible customers. And unlinking a project's billing account stops its paid services, so resources may be deleted.

= table sm | Billing roles | Six roles, three different grant points

`billing.admin` Administrator | Billing account | Everything: users, payment, links, budgets, exports
`billing.user` User | Billing account | **Link projects to this account**

N: The billing roles. Notice the middle column, because the grant point matters. On the billing account: Administrator can do everything. User can link projects to this account. Viewer reads costs. Costs Manager handles budgets, exports and cost views, with no control of payment. At the organization: Billing Account Creator creates new billing accounts. And on the project: Project Billing Manager links and unlinks that project.

= dia | The most examined billing fact | Linking a project needs a grant on both sides

On the right, Billing Account User on the billing account. Look at the two arrows. Owner of the project alone cannot attach it to an account it has no role on.
V: Linking needs a grant on both sides: the project and the billing account.

## 2.8 Reading and predicting cost

= table | Which cost tool | Four questions, four tools

= table | Discounts | Commitment against flexibility
Mechanism | Commitment | Notes
**Sustained use** | None, automatic | Eligible series running much of the month. E2 and several newer series get none (*verify*)
**Committed use, resource-based** | 1 or 3 years of vCPU, memory or GPU in a region | Deepest for a steady footprint; billed whether used or not
**Committed use, spend-based** | 1 or 3 years of hourly spend | Flexible across machine types and regions
**Spot VMs** | None | Large discount; can be reclaimed at any time
**Free Trial** | None | $300 credit for 90 days (*verify*)
N: The discounts. Sustained use discounts are automatic, with no commitment, for eligible machine series running much of the month. E2 and several newer series do not receive them, and that changes, so verify. Resource-based committed use discounts commit you to one or three years of V-C-P-U, memory or G-P-U in a region. They give the deepest discount for a steady footprint, and you pay whether you use them or not. Spend-based committed use discounts commit to hourly spend, and are flexible across machine types and regions. Spot V-Ms need no commitment and carry a large discount, but they can be reclaimed at any time.

= panel tip | Three phrases | How to spot the discount
*"Steady, predictable, 24×7 for years"*: **committed use discount**, three years if the stem says so.
*"Interruptible, restartable, cheapest"*: **Spot VMs**.
*"No commitment, discount without doing anything"*: **sustained use discount**.
N: How to spot them. Steady, predictable, twenty-four seven for years: committed use discount, three years if the stem says so. Interruptible, restartable, cheapest: Spot V-Ms. No commitment, a discount without doing anything: sustained use discount.

= table | Three kinds of metadata | Labels are the cost dimension
Metadata | What it is | In billing export? | Used by
**Labels** | Free key-value pairs on resources | **Yes** | Cost attribution, filtering, automation
**Network tags** | Strings on VMs | **No** | VPC firewall rules and routes
**Tags** (resource-manager) | IAM-governed key-values, inherited | Not their job | Conditional IAM, org policy, Cloud NGFW
N: Three kinds of metadata, with confusing names. Labels are free key-value pairs on resources. They appear in the billing export, so labels are the cost dimension. Network tags are plain strings on V-Ms, used by V-P-C firewall rules and routes. They do not appear in billing. And Tags, also called resource-manager tags, are key-value pairs governed by I-A-M, attached to organizations, folders, projects and resources, and inherited. They drive conditional I-A-M, organization policy and Cloud Next Generation Firewall. We return to them in parts three and four.

## 2.9 Seeing everything: Cloud Asset Inventory and Gemini Cloud Assist

= cmd sm | Cloud Asset Inventory | The answer whenever you need a view across projects
Need | Command or capability
Every VM with an external IP, across the org | gcloud asset search-all-resources --scope=organizations/ORG --query="..."

N: Cloud Asset Inventory is the metadata database of every resource and policy in your organization. It is the answer whenever a question needs a view across projects. ## Part 2 sample questions

= th | Part 2 · practice | Eight exam-style questions | Listen, use the silence, name the deciding clause.
N: Eight questions on the hierarchy, billing and quotas. As before, try to name the phrase that decides each one.

= q | A startup signs up for a Free Trial with a personal Google account and sees a resource named `lara-brown-org` above its project. A co-founder needs organization-level administration. What is true?
(A) It is a Cloud Identity organization; verify the company domain and rename it
(B) It is a standalone organization; invite the co-founder as an owner, who must accept within 30 days
(C) Organizations need Workspace; this resource is a placeholder
(D) Grant the co-founder `roles/owner` on the project; org roles do not apply to trial accounts
ans B
why A :: A standalone org's name cannot be a domain.
why B :: New Free Trial customers get one automatically, and people join by invitation.
why C :: False: a standalone org is a real organization.
why D :: Project Owner is not organization administration.
C: The answer is B. The clues are Free Trial, and a name built from the user, not a domain. That is a standalone organization, and people join by an invitation accepted within thirty days. A fails, since a standalone organization's name can never be a domain. C is false. And D gives project Owner, which is not administration of the organization.

= q | Finance needs read access to all of its projects, including projects created next year. There are 40 projects in the `finance` folder today. What should you do?
(A) Bind a role to each user on each of the 40 projects with a script
(B) Bind a role to the finance group on the `finance` folder
(C) Bind `roles/viewer` to the finance group on the organization
(D) Create a custom role at the folder level
ans B
why A :: Per-user, per-project, and it misses new projects.
why B :: One group binding, inherited by every current and future project.
why C :: Far too broad: it reaches every project in the organization.
why D :: Custom roles cannot be defined at a folder.
C: The answer is B. The deciding clause is, including projects created next year. Inheritance from the folder covers the future, and a group means people are managed by membership. A works today but fails tomorrow. C is overly broad, since the organization reaches everything. D has a fact to learn: custom roles exist at the organization or project level, never at a folder.

= q | An engineer is Owner of a new project, but gets a permission error linking it to the company billing account. What is the most likely missing grant?
(A) Billing Account User on the billing account
(B) Project Billing Manager on the project
(C) Billing Account Viewer on the billing account
(D) Organization Administrator on the organization
ans A
why A :: Linking needs a grant on both sides, and the billing side is missing.
why B :: Owner already covers the project side.
why C :: Viewer reads costs but cannot link.
why D :: Far too broad, and it is not a billing role.
C: The answer is A. This is the two-sided rule. Owner already covers the project side, so the missing permission is on the billing account: Billing Account User. B is therefore redundant. C reads costs and cannot link. D is the kind of over-broad answer that least privilege eliminates.

= q | Leadership wants development spend to **never** exceed a limit on BigQuery queries. A warning is not enough. What should you do?
(A) Create a budget with thresholds at 50, 90 and 100%
(B) Lower the BigQuery query usage per day quota on the project
(C) Set a billing export to BigQuery and review it each morning
(D) Ask Gemini Cloud Assist to monitor the costs
ans B
why A :: A budget only notifies.
why B :: A quota is a hard stop, and it can be lowered on purpose.
why C :: A report after the fact stops nothing.
why D :: An assistant answers questions and enforces nothing.
C: The answer is B. The deciding word is never, followed by, a warning is not enough. A quota is a hard stop, and you may lower one deliberately. A is the classic distractor, since a budget only notifies. C and D observe the spending, but neither of them enforces anything.

= q | A project hits its regional limit on GPUs, and the workload is legitimate and approved. What is the recommended action?
(A) Create more projects and spread the workload across them
(B) Request a quota increase for that GPU quota in that project
(C) Move the workload to a different billing account
(D) Raise the budget for the project
ans B
why A :: Spreading to dodge a quota is the distractor.
why B :: Quotas are per project, and increases go through Cloud Quotas.
why C :: Billing accounts do not hold compute quota.
why D :: A budget has no effect on quota.
C: The answer is B. Quotas are per project and often per region, and a legitimate need gets an increase request in Cloud Quotas. A is the tempting workaround, and it is the wrong one. C and D confuse money with capacity.

= q | A security team must list every VM with an external IP across 300 projects, without writing a script that loops over projects. What should they use?
(A) `gcloud compute instances list` in each project
(B) Cloud Asset Inventory, `search-all-resources` at the organization scope
(C) The billing export to BigQuery
(D) VPC Flow Logs
ans B
why A :: This is exactly the per-project loop they want to avoid.
why B :: A single query across the whole organization.
why C :: Billing data is about cost, not current configuration.
why D :: Flow logs show traffic, not which VMs have external IPs.
C: The answer is B. The deciding phrase is across three hundred projects. Whenever a question needs a view across projects, think Cloud Asset Inventory, with the search scoped to the organization. A is the loop they wanted to avoid. C holds cost data. And D holds traffic samples, not an inventory of configuration.

= q | A finance analyst needs a monthly report of spend per team. Every resource already has a `team` label. Which **two** steps deliver this with the least effort? Choose two.
(A) Enable the standard usage cost billing export to BigQuery
(B) Query the export, grouping by the `team` label
(C) Attach network tags with team names to every VM
(D) Create one billing account per team
(E) Create a budget per team, and read the alert emails
ans A, B
why A :: The export carries labels into a queryable table.
why B :: Labels are the cost dimension.
why C :: Network tags do not appear in the billing export.
why D :: Heavy reorganization, and labels already exist.
why E :: Budgets alert; they are not a report.
C: The answers are A and B. Labels are the cost dimension, and the export brings them into BigQuery, so you group by the label. C is the classic metadata trap: network tags are for firewall rules, and they are not in the billing export. D reorganizes billing to solve something labels already solve. E gives alerts, not a report. And remember, on a choose two item, exactly two selections are needed.

= q | Your organization's users keep creating projects and billing accounts outside the approved folder structure. What is the best first governance step?
(A) Delete the unapproved projects each week
(B) Remove the default Project Creator and Billing Account Creator grants at the organization
(C) Set a budget on each billing account
(D) Enforce `compute.skipDefaultNetworkCreation`
ans B
why A :: Treats the symptom, forever.
why B :: Those defaults are why anyone in the domain can do this.
why C :: Budgets do not stop project or account creation.
why D :: Skips a network; it does not limit who creates projects.
C: The answer is B. When a Cloud Identity or Workspace organization is created, every user in the domain receives those two roles at the organization. Remove them, and then grant Project Creator on specific folders to the people who need it. A treats the symptom. C is a budget again. And D is a real baseline, but it controls the default network, not who creates projects.

## Part 2: frequently missed patterns

= th | Part 2 · more practice | Environments, cost and inventory | Every one of these hinges on where a boundary sits.

= q | A new organisation's foundation will use Shared VPC. Security requires that no misconfigured route or firewall rule in development or test can ever affect production traffic. How should the host projects be laid out?
(A) One host project with one VPC, and one subnet per environment
(B) One host project per application team, shared by all its environments
(C) No Shared VPC: a separate VPC in every project, peered together
(D) A production host project and a separate non-production host project
ans D
why A :: Subnets in one VPC share its routes and firewall rules, so a mistake in one environment can affect another.
why B :: Per-team hosts mix environments inside each team's network.
why C :: Peering every project rebuilds the many-networks problem Shared VPC removes.
why D :: Correct. Each environment's network is a separate VPC with its own routes and rules, administered centrally.
C: The answer is D. Routes and firewall rules belong to a VPC. Separate host projects, each with its own VPC, mean a change in non-production can't reach production.

= q | Five teams share one GKE Standard cluster, each in its own namespace. Finance already has the detailed billing export in BigQuery but sees only node costs. It needs cost per namespace. What should you enable?
(A) A label on each node pool with the team's name
(B) A separate billing account for each team
(C) GKE cost allocation on the cluster
(D) A budget for each namespace in Cloud Billing
ans C
why A :: The teams share node pools, so node pool labels can't separate their costs.
why B :: Billing accounts attach to projects, not namespaces.
why C :: Correct. Cost allocation adds namespace and label detail to the export rows.
why D :: Budgets can't be scoped to a namespace, and they don't produce the cost breakdown.
C: The answer is C. With GKE cost allocation, the billing export breaks cluster costs down by namespace and Kubernetes labels, based on the resources each workload requests.

= q | You need to see what the configuration of the firewall rule allow-admin looked like ten days ago, before someone widened its source range. No feeds or exports were ever configured. What can you use?
(A) The rule's asset history in Cloud Asset Inventory
(B) Firewall Insights for the rule
(C) VPC Flow Logs for the subnets the rule applies to
(D) A new Cloud Asset Inventory feed for firewall rules
ans A
why A :: Correct. Cloud Asset Inventory keeps five weeks of history for resource metadata and IAM policies, so the rule's history (gcloud asset get-history) returns earlier versions without any setup.
why B :: Firewall Insights reports shadowed and unused rules and hit counts, not earlier configurations.
why C :: Flow logs record traffic, not a rule's configuration, and nobody enabled them.
why D :: Feeds send notifications for future changes only.
C: The answer is A. Cloud Asset Inventory keeps five weeks of history for resource metadata and IAM policies, so get-history returns earlier versions without any setup.

= q | Deleting the Shared VPC host project at Ingram Logistics would take down every service project's network. Several administrators hold Owner on it. Using generally available features only, how do you stop anyone from deleting the project, Owners included?
(A) Remove the Owner role from everyone and grant Editor instead
(B) A deny policy on the project that denies the projects.delete permission
(C) An organisation policy that restricts resource locations for the project
(D) A budget with a 100% threshold that alerts the network team
ans B
why A :: Editor can't delete a project, but removing Owner from everyone also removes the administrators' ability to run the project.
why B :: Correct. A deny rule on projects.delete stops every principal it names, including Owners, and it is GA.
why C :: A location policy limits where resources are created, not whether the project can be deleted.
why D :: A budget watches spending; it can't block an action.
C: The answer is B. Deny policies are evaluated before allow policies, so a deny rule for cloudresourcemanager.googleapis.com/projects.delete blocks deletion whatever roles a principal holds.

= q | A manager at Thursby Media has Billing Account Viewer and asks why last month's bill was 30% higher than the month before, and which projects and services drove it. They don't write SQL, and only generally available features may be used. What should they use?
(A) The detailed billing export, queried in BigQuery
(B) A budget with alerts at 50%, 90% and 100%
(C) Cloud Billing reports, grouped by project and then by service, comparing the two months
(D) The Cost Table filtered by invoice month, exported to CSV
ans C
why A :: The export answers the question but needs SQL, which the manager doesn't write.
why B :: A budget alerts about future spending; it doesn't explain last month's increase.
why C :: Correct. Grouping the reports by project and by service shows which ones grew between the two months.
why D :: The cost table lists invoice lines, but comparing two months in a spreadsheet is the slow way.
C: The answer is C. Billing reports chart cost over time grouped by project, service or SKU, and can compare periods, so the manager sees what grew without writing queries.
